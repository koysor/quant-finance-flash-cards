# Fisher Equation

**Topic:** Financial Mathematics
**Tags:** fisher equation, nominal rate, real rate, inflation, break-even inflation, interest rates

## Definition

The **Fisher equation** states that a nominal interest rate is approximately the sum of a real interest rate and expected inflation. It explains why nominal bond yields contain an inflation premium and is the key link between monetary policy, bond markets, and inflation expectations.

## Key Formula

**Exact form:**

$$(1 + r_{\text{nom}}) = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + \pi)$$

**Linearised approximation** (valid when rates are small):

$$r_{\text{nom}} \approx r_{\text{real}} + \pi$$

**Break-even inflation** (from market prices):

$$\pi^* = r_{\text{nom}} - r_{\text{real}} = \text{nominal yield} - \text{TIPS yield}$$

where $r_{\text{nom}}$ is the nominal yield (conventional bond) and $r_{\text{real}}$ is the real yield (inflation-linked bond of the same maturity).

## Example

A 10-year US Treasury yields 4.50% and the 10-year TIPS yields 1.80%. The break-even inflation rate is:

$$\pi^* = 4.50\% - 1.80\% = 2.70\%$$

The market is pricing in 2.70% average annual inflation over the next 10 years. If actual inflation exceeds 2.70%, the TIPS holder benefits; if it falls short, the conventional Treasury holder wins.

## Remember

Break-even inflation (the Fisher spread) is watched closely by central banks as a real-time gauge of inflation expectations. When the Fed raises rates, nominal yields rise; if real yields rise equally, break-evens are unchanged (purely a real tightening). If nominal yields rise but break-evens also rise, the market is pricing in higher future inflation — a warning that the rate rise may be insufficient to anchor expectations. Inflation-linked bond traders use the Fisher equation daily to decide whether to hold nominal or real bonds.
